In this episode, I shared a few early-season thoughts from a new vantage point: life in the Bay Area, watching games at Oracle Park, and following one of the more interesting pitching-development stories of the season in Colorado.

## Watching Baseball from the Bay

After moving to the Bay Area, I finally had the chance to watch the Giants in person at Oracle Park. My seat was in the upper deck behind home plate, which turned out to be a perfect place to take in the full geometry of the game. From that angle, the field opens up cleanly, and beyond the outfield you get the signature view of the bay.

![Oracle Park from the upper deck behind home plate](/images/oracle-park-opening-day-2026.jpg)

*Oracle Park on Opening Day, viewed from the upper deck behind home plate.*

The Opening Day presentation was also memorable. The atmosphere had the familiar rhythm of a ballpark celebration, but what stood out most to me was how much live information is now built into the stadium experience. The scoreboard was not just showing the traditional line score. It displayed Statcast-style information such as hitter exit velocity, pitcher spin rate, pitch movement, and even defensive metrics like exchange time and arm strength.

That changed how I watched the game. Seeing those numbers immediately after each play makes the stadium feel like a live analytics lab. A hard-hit ball, a pitch with late movement, or a quick transfer from an infielder no longer lives only in the eye test; the park gives fans the data layer almost instantly.

## The Rockies' Pitching Experiment

We also spent time talking about the Rockies, whose pitching staff has been one of the more interesting early-season stories. As Jared Greenspan summarized for [MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/news/rockies-modernized-pitching-changes-2026), Colorado made sweeping changes after several difficult seasons, including new pitching leadership and a more modern approach to pitch design.

The biggest change is pitch-mix diversification. The Rockies are no longer leaning as heavily on four-seam fastballs. According to MLB.com, their four-seam usage dropped from 40.9% last season to 29.1% early in 2026. That is a meaningful philosophical shift, especially for an organization that has long had to solve pitching in the unique environment of Coors Field.

The staff has also been adding weapons. MLB.com noted that six of the seven relievers on the active roster had debuted a new pitch, and the current staff had combined to add 12 new pitches from the previous season. For fantasy baseball, that matters because a new pitch can change a pitcher's shape of outcomes: more whiffs, better platoon answers, softer contact, or simply a path to staying in the strike zone with more confidence.

What I found most interesting is that the changes are not random experimentation. The Rockies appear to be matching pitch ideas to each pitcher's body and throwing mechanics. The article specifically discussed adjusting recommendations based on whether a pitcher is more of a pronator or supinator, along with arm slot and pitch shape. That is the modern pitch-design mindset: not every pitcher should chase the same sweeper, sinker, or cutter. The best pitch is the one that fits the player's movement pattern and gives hitters a different problem to solve.

For fantasy purposes, this is why I think the Rockies are worth monitoring more closely than usual. The park context is still difficult, and we should not overreact to one month. But when an organization changes its process this visibly--fewer fastballs, more individualized arsenals, more biomechanics-driven pitch design--it creates chances for pitchers to break old expectations.
